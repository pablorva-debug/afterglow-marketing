The reconstruction guide

How to find every concert you've been to

You remember the night. You've forgotten the year, the support act, and whether that venue was even called that back then. Most of it is still recoverable — scattered across six places you already have access to. Here's the order to work through them.

Updated August 2026  ·  9 min read  ·  By the Afterglow team

Before you start

Why your gig memory has holes in it

Concert memory decays in a specific, predictable way. The night survives; the metadata doesn't. You'll remember standing three rows back and the exact moment the lights went down, and have no idea whether it was 2013 or 2015. Venues get renamed and rebranded. Support acts disappear from memory within about a year. Tickets bought by whoever in your group was quickest never landed in your inbox at all.

The good news is that you have been leaving a paper trail this entire time without meaning to. Every ticket purchase generated a confirmation email. Every gig generated a cluster of terrible dark photographs. Most generated a bar tab. Reconstruction isn't a memory exercise — it's a search exercise, and it's largely mechanical.

Work in the order below. The first two steps are where the overwhelming majority of your history lives, and they're the ones that give you exact dates. Everything after that is for filling gaps.

Set aside an evening, open a blank spreadsheet with four columns — date, artist, venue, city — and don't stop to tidy as you go. Dump everything in first, deduplicate at the end.

The method

Seven places your concert history is hiding

Ordered by yield per minute spent. Steps one and two will get most people to something like eighty per cent of their history.

Step 01

Ticket vendor order histories

Start here, always. A vendor order history hands you the date, the venue and the headliner in one row, already correct — no cross-referencing needed. Log into every ticketing account you've ever made, including the ones you'd forgotten about.

  • Ticketmaster — My Account, then My Tickets or Order History. Note that Ticketmaster runs separate regional accounts: if you've lived in more than one country, or moved between the UK and US sites, you may have two account histories under the same email.
  • AXS — My Account, then My Events. Past events are usually kept in a separate tab from upcoming ones, so check both.
  • DICE — in the app, the Tickets tab has a past events section, and DICE is unusually good about keeping the full history rather than expiring it. If you've been going to small and mid-size shows in the UK for the last decade, this is a goldmine.
  • Eventbrite — the Tickets page lists every order back to account creation. Strong for club nights, all-dayers, warehouse events and anything promoter-run.
  • See Tickets — My Account has an order history, and there's also a Find My Order lookup if you bought as a guest and never made an account. That lookup needs your email and order reference, which is a good reason to do the email step next.

Worth checking too, depending on what you're into and where you live: Resident Advisor for electronic and club events, Skiddle and Gigantic in the UK, Ticketweb for small venues, and your local venue's own box office system — independent venues often run their own ticketing and hold years of history you won't find anywhere else.

Expect a ceiling here. Several vendors only display the last few years online even though the purchase is still in their records. That's not a dead end — it's what makes step two necessary.
Step 02

Your email archive

Email reaches back further than any order history, because you kept it and they didn't. If you've held the same address since your twenties, this step alone can recover a decade nobody else can give you. The trick is searching by sender and date range rather than by keyword, and working through it two years at a time so the results stay readable.

Gmail

from:(ticketmaster OR axs OR dice OR eventbrite OR seetickets OR skiddle OR ticketweb) in:anywhere subject:(tickets OR "order confirmation" OR "your order") after:2014/01/01 before:2016/01/01 "e-ticket" OR "eticket" OR "print at home" has:attachment (doors OR "support act" OR "stage times") in:anywhere

in:anywhere is the important one — it includes archived mail, spam and trash, which is where a lot of ten-year-old confirmations have quietly ended up. Change the date bounds and run the second search again for each two-year block.

Outlook and Outlook.com

from:ticketmaster received:2014-01-01..2015-12-31 subject:tickets hasattachment:yes

If you use Apple Mail or a desktop client, the same logic applies through its own search fields — sender plus a date window, repeated in blocks. And if you've changed email addresses over the years, dig out the old account. That's usually where the earliest and most interesting half of your history is sitting.

Delivery emails count too. A vinyl order placed the morning after a show, a hotel booking in a city you don't live in, a train ticket to Manchester on a Saturday — these date gigs indirectly when the ticket confirmation itself has vanished.
Step 03

Listening data, for the ones you can only half remember

This step doesn't find shows — it dates them. Almost everybody listens to an artist obsessively in the week before seeing them, and again the morning after. That double spike is a fingerprint, and it turns "sometime around 2017" into a specific week.

  • Last.fm — if you've scrobbled for any length of time, open an artist in your library and look at the scrobble chart over time. The spikes are the gigs. Last.fm also ran an events and attendance feature years ago, so if you were an early user there may be old marked-attending data still attached to your profile.
  • Spotify — go to Privacy Settings in your account and request your extended streaming history. It takes a few days to arrive and covers your entire account life, timestamped to the minute. Overkill for casual use, genuinely powerful for reconstruction.
  • Apple Music and YouTube Music — both let you request your data through the equivalent privacy settings, though the exports are less granular than Spotify's.

Once you have a candidate week, jump to step seven to convert it into an exact date.

Step 04

Your photo library, filtered by place

This is the best source for everything you never bought a ticket for — free shows, festival side stages, support slots you turned up early for, guest list nights, that band you watched in the back room of a pub. The photos are dated and, usually, geotagged.

  • Google Photos — use the Places view to browse by location, or search a venue name directly; Google is surprisingly good at recognising well-known venues. Then narrow by year.
  • Apple Photos — the Places album shows a map of everywhere you've taken a photo. Zoom into the streets your city's venues are on and work through the pins.
  • Look for the signature — a cluster of dark, blurry, badly framed shots taken between 9pm and 11pm, plus one shaky video with distorted audio. That's a gig, every time.
Photos give you the date reliably and the artist unreliably. Note what you can, mark it uncertain, and confirm the lineup in step seven.
Step 05

Bank and card statements

Statements catch the purchases that never generated an email: box office walk-ups, cash-free door entry, tickets a friend bought that you paid back. Most banks keep five to seven years of searchable history, and most banking apps let you export it as a spreadsheet.

  • Search transactions for the vendor names — Ticketmaster, DICE, AXS, Eventbrite, See Tickets.
  • Search for venue names directly. Venue bars, cloakrooms and merch stands nearly always trade under the venue's own name.
  • Scan for late-night taxi and transport charges near venue postcodes. A cab home at 11:40pm from a street you have no other reason to be on is usually a gig.

Bank transfers to friends are worth a look as well — a payment labelled with an artist's name and nothing else is exactly the kind of evidence that unlocks a show you'd completely lost.

Step 06

Old Facebook event RSVPs

For anyone who was going out between roughly 2009 and 2018, Facebook events are the definitive record of the small-venue and club era — the promoter-run nights that never touched a major ticketing platform.

  • On your profile, open the Events section and look for past events. The interface for this has moved repeatedly over the years, so if you can't find it, use the second option.
  • Request a copy of your information from Facebook's settings and choose the Events category. The export includes your RSVPs with dates, which is far more complete than what the interface shows.
Set expectations here: this is the source most likely to disappoint. Events get deleted when promoter pages are taken down, and interest data was never guaranteed to persist. Take what's there and move on.
Step 07

Cross-reference, then lock it down

You should now have a messy list: some rows exact, some rows saying "Warpaint, probably spring 2017, that place near the station". This step converts the second kind into the first.

  • setlist.fm — the deepest crowd-sourced archive of what was played where and when. Search the artist, find the tour, find the date they played your city. A tour usually only passes through a given city once, so the year plus the city is often all it takes. It'll also tell you the support act you'd forgotten and, frequently, jog loose a memory of the night itself. If you use it a lot, our guide to setlist.fm and what sits alongside it is worth a look.
  • The venue's own past events archive — most venue websites keep a listings archive going back years, and it's the fastest way to confirm a date when you're sure of the place but not the night.
  • Artist tour pages and Wikipedia — full tour date lists for anyone big enough to have them, which settles the year instantly.

What to record for each show

Keep it to four fields, and resist the urge to add more until the list is finished: date, artist, venue, city. That's the minimum that makes a list portable between a spreadsheet, an app, or whatever you use in fifteen years. If you want to add colour later, the two things people most regret not writing down are the support acts and who they went with.

Where you're genuinely unsure, write the date as the month rather than inventing a day. A honest "May 2016" is more useful in a decade than a confident wrong Tuesday.

Honest limits

What you won't get back

No method recovers everything, and a guide that claims otherwise is selling you something. Here's where the trail genuinely goes cold — and why it matters less than you'd think.

The gaps

Anything paid for in cash before you owned a smartphone leaves no digital trace at all. Festivals collapse into a single ticket, so the forty sets you actually watched are gone unless you photographed them. Support acts are the first thing to vanish and the hardest to recover, though setlist.fm rescues more of them than you'd expect. Tickets someone else bought never appear in your accounts. And a lot of the best nights — free shows, guest list, a mate's band in a pub back room — were never transactions in the first place.

The realistic target is a strong, mostly complete list rather than a perfect one — and the honest reason to build it now is that the trail keeps getting colder. Platforms shut down, accounts get deleted, phone backups fail. Every year you leave it, a bit more goes. Once you've got the list out of your head and into a file, it stops decaying — and from that point on you're only ever adding to it. Common questions

Reconstructing your gig history — FAQ

How far back can I realistically go?

If you've kept the same email address, roughly as far back as you've been buying tickets online — for most people, the mid-2000s. Order histories on the ticketing sites often only show recent years, but the confirmation emails usually survive much longer. Anything bought at a box office with cash before you had a smartphone is memory and photographs only.

What if I only remember the year?

Narrow the window with listening data or photos, then look up the artist's tour on setlist.fm or their own tour archive. A tour usually only passes through a given city once, so the year plus the city is often enough to pin the exact date.

Can Spotify or Apple Music tell me which concerts I've been to?

No — neither one records attendance. What they can do is date things. Spotify's extended streaming history, requested through your privacy settings, shows exactly what you played and when, so a listening spike around a date you can't place is strong evidence you had tickets.

Is there an app that imports my concert history automatically?

Not reliably, and be sceptical of anything claiming otherwise. Ticket vendors don't give third-party apps access to your order history, so a "complete automatic import" is either inbox scraping or marketing. Reconstruction is a manual job. What an app can usefully do is give the finished list somewhere permanent to live and keep growing.

Where should I keep the list once I've built it?

Build it in a spreadsheet first — it's portable and nobody can take it away from you.
