The analogy, examined

Letterboxd, but for gigs

It's the phrase everyone reaches for, and it's a good one — right up until it isn't. You can rewatch a film. You cannot rewatch a Tuesday night in 2019. Almost everything interesting about logging concerts follows from that one difference.

Updated August 2026  ·  4 min read  ·  By the Afterglow team

Why the phrase caught on

What people actually mean by it

Letterboxd worked out something specific: the act of logging changes the thing you're logging. Knowing you'll write down a film makes you watch it slightly differently. The diary accumulates into something you didn't set out to build, and then one day it's a record of a decade of your life that happens to be organised by films.

When people ask for a Letterboxd for gigs, they're asking for that — not a ticketing app, not a notifications feed. A place where the record is the point, it looks decent, and your friends are in it.

Nobody's asking for another app that tells them Fontaines D.C. are playing. That problem is solved. The unsolved one is what happens to the show afterwards.

The interesting part

Where the analogy snaps

Three differences that aren't just missing features. They change what the log is for.

One

A film waits for you. A gig doesn't.

You can watch any film ever made tonight. Which means a Letterboxd profile is a record of taste — of what you chose, in what order, at your own pace. Nobody can attend a show that already happened. So a concert log is a record of presence: of where you physically were, on a specific night, having spent the money and made the journey. It's autobiography with timestamps rather than a curated shelf.

Two

Which is exactly why verification matters

Claiming you've seen a film is unfalsifiable and nobody cares — worst case you're a bit of a fraud at a dinner party. Claiming you were at a legendary show is a different kind of claim, because it couldn't be repeated and it can't be checked. Every concert app runs on self-declared attendance: you tick a box, it believes you. A GPS check-in made at the venue is a different sort of statement from a ticked box — and the reason we're strict about it is that shows you add later, retrospectively, aren't marked verified either. We weren't there. The stamp only means something if it can't be given away.

Three

There's no TMDB for live music

Letterboxd stands on a complete, structured film database. Live music has nothing equivalent. Setlist.fm has the deepest archive going, but it only holds shows someone bothered to submit a setlist for — which quietly excludes club nights, DJ sets, side stages and most of what a normal year of gig-going actually consists of. Any honest concert app has to let you log a show that exists in no database at all, because most of yours do.

Straight answer

So what's actually closest?

Nothing is Letterboxd. Concert Archives gets closest on substance — running since 2013, millions of concerts documented, plus setlists, bucket lists, friend follows and flashback reminders. It'll happily take a decade of backlog. Our comparison covers it properly.

Afterglow is our attempt at the other half: the diary, the ratings, the friends, a page you'd actually share — plus a verified stamp, which is the one thing films never needed. What we don't have is the lists culture, the reviewing community, or anything approaching Letterboxd's fifteen-year head start. If you want to see how we stack up against everything else, the honest roundup puts us last on purpose.

Starting from an empty diary is the worst part of any of these apps. If you've got years of shows to recover first, our guide to finding every concert you've been to gets most of them back out of records you already have.

Common questions

Letterboxd for concerts — FAQ

Is there a Letterboxd for concerts?

Not feature for feature. Concert Archives is closest overall — the deepest database, plus a diary, friends and flashbacks. Afterglow adds the one thing nothing else has: verified check-ins. Neither has the lists or reviewing culture that make Letterboxd what it is.

Can I rate concerts like films?

You can rate shows in Afterglow. Worth knowing that the number means something messier than a film rating — the sound, the crowd, the venue, who you went with and what kind of week you were having all end up inside it. That's not a flaw. It's arguably the point.

Is there a watchlist equivalent?

Not really, because you can't decide to attend something that already happened. The closest thing is seeing which upcoming shows your friends are going to. For the forward-looking side — artist alerts, tour announcements — Bandsintown does it better than we do, and we don't have artist alerts at all yet.

Why can't I just import my history like a Letterboxd CSV?

Because there's nothing to import from. Ticket vendors don't give third-party apps access to your order history, and no central record of your attendance exists anywhere. Every app on this subject involves typing. We'd rather say that plainly than surprise you after signup.
